abstract: |
  What would happen to European bank balance sheets if Europe adopted the American loan loss provisioning standard, the Current Expected Credit Loss (CECL) framework? This question sits at the intersection of two live policy debates: the ongoing divergence between US and EU bank accounting regimes following the 2008 financial crisis, and the broader question of whether technical accounting rules generate real economic consequences or merely relabel existing balance sheet realities.

  This chapter provides the first causal estimates of CECL's impact on bank reserves using the synthetic control method (SCM), and uses those estimates to construct a quantified, empirically grounded counterfactual for European adoption. Exploiting the quasi-experimental variation created by US banks adopting CECL in January 2020 while European banks continued operating under IFRS 9 since 2018, I match each of 116 US commercial banks to a weighted combination of European peers with similar pre-treatment provisioning trajectories. A critical challenge is that CECL adoption coincided exactly with the COVID-19 pandemic; I address this using in-time placebo tests that assign fake treatment dates to the same US banks rather than contaminated in-space placebos drawn from COVID-affected European institutions.

  The main finding is that CECL increased loan loss reserve ratios by 0.32 percentage points on average during 2020–2024, a 25% rise relative to the pre-adoption mean of 1.27%, implying approximately $17 billion in additional US bank reserves. This aggregate, however, is shaped almost entirely by the behaviour of medium and large banks: medium-sized institutions (assets $20–80bn) increased reserves by 0.87pp, and large banks (assets >$80bn) by 0.43pp, with both effects significant at the 5% level. The European policy implication follows directly: since European banks are on average larger than their American counterparts, applying these size-stratified estimates to the eurozone loan base of €13.5 trillion implies €39 billion in additional reserves — roughly 0.33% of eurozone GDP and under half the capital impact attributed to Basel III.

  Against this backdrop, the most striking finding is the behaviour of small banks. Institutions with assets below $20 billion decreased reserves by 0.27pp on average, a highly significant effect running opposite to the standard's design intent. This result is not paradoxical: CECL's simplified implementation methods, applied to small banks' short-maturity portfolios, frequently yield lower lifetime ECL estimates than the conservative incurred-loss provisions that accumulated under post-2008 regulatory pressure. Whether a bank acted on that lower estimate depended on its capital position. Cross-sectional analysis shows that loan portfolio composition — mortgage share, corporate loan share, consumer loan share — has no explanatory power for heterogeneity within the small bank tercile. The dominant predictor is the Tier 1 capital ratio (significant at the 1% level): well-capitalised small banks, unconstrained by binding regulatory thresholds and shielded from immediate capital costs by the CARES Act transition relief, used CECL's methodological flexibility to minimise reported provisions. Banks with tighter capital positions and higher NPL ratios provisioned upward, as the standard intended.
